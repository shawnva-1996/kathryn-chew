**The short answer:** an Option to Purchase expires on the exact date and time printed on it, and if you don't exercise it by then, the seller keeps your option fee. Don't rely on anyone's memory of that date, including your agent's. One couple lost **$30,000** because their agent remembered "21 days" when the Option said 14, and the document sat unread in an office drawer.

---

## The Option in the drawer

Mr and Mrs J were selling their condo and buying a landed home. They found one at **$3 million**.

They paid a 1% option fee, **$30,000**, and the seller issued the Option to Purchase. Their agent, Mr H, collected it and handed it to Mr J at his office. Mr J put it in his desk drawer.

The Option was clear. It was valid for **14 days**, until 4pm on the last day. If not exercised by then, the $30,000 would be forfeited.

Mr H didn't keep a copy. He didn't note the date.

Then a boundary issue came up on the land, and the J's asked for a lower price. With the clock running, Mr J messaged Mr H: was he supposed to sign something today?

Mr H replied: *"OTP 21 days."*

Mr J pointed out it had been changed to 14 days. Mr H didn't reply. The seller's agent asked Mr H the same day whether his clients would exercise or end things amicably. He didn't answer that either.

The Option expired the next afternoon.

Three days later, the J's took the Option out of the drawer to exercise it. It was already dead. The seller refused to extend it.

## What it cost

| Who | What happened |
|---|---|
| **Mr and Mrs J, the buyers** | Lost the **$30,000** option fee and the home. Mr H later repaid **$5,000**, so their net loss was **$25,000**. |
| **Mr H, their agent** | Fined **$2,500**, plus $2,000 costs. |

The Disciplinary Committee noted something uncomfortable. The date was not technical. Anyone could have read it on the document the buyers were holding.

## The moment that matters

The Option was never hidden. It was in Mr J's own drawer the whole time.

The problem wasn't missing information. It was *where the buyers were looking for it*. They asked a person when they should have looked at the paper.

## What to avoid

**1. Don't treat your agent's memory as the source.**
The document is the source. Everyone, including your agent, is working from memory unless they're reading it.

**2. Don't file the Option away without a calendar entry.**
The moment you receive it, put the expiry date and time in your phone, with a reminder two or three days before.

**3. Don't let a dispute run down the clock.**
A negotiation over price or defects does not pause the Option. Decide what you'll do before the deadline, not after.

**4. Don't accept silence on a deadline question.**
If you ask "when does this expire?" and get no clear answer, read the document yourself that same day.

## What to do instead

The day you receive an Option to Purchase:

- **Read the expiry date and time yourself** and write them down.
- **Make two copies:** one for you, one for your agent, and ask your lawyer to confirm the date too.
- **Set reminders** before the expiry, early enough to reach your lawyer and bank.
- **If you're negotiating anything during the option period,** ask your lawyer what happens to the deadline, and get any extension in writing from the seller.
- **Know exactly how to exercise it:** what to sign, what to pay, and where it goes.

## What would change this answer

The option period, fee and exercise steps are whatever the Option itself says, and they can differ between private property and HDB resale. Your lawyer can confirm the terms of yours. An extension only exists if the seller agrees to it in writing.

## Quick questions

**What happens if my Option to Purchase expires?**
If you don't exercise it in time, the seller can keep your option fee, and you lose the right to buy.

**Can the seller extend the Option?**
Only if they agree. In this case, the seller refused.

**Does a dispute over the property stop the deadline?**
No. Unless the seller agrees otherwise in writing, the expiry date stays the same.

**Who should keep track of the date?**
You, your agent and your lawyer. But only you can be sure you've read it yourself.
