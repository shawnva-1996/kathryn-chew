**The short answer:** compare the asking price with recent sales of the **same size** in the **same project** first. Then look at floor level, and only then at nearby projects. Ignore the project's overall average, because it mixes small units with big ones. And if there are only one or two recent sales, the honest answer is "there isn't enough evidence yet". That is not the same as "it's a fair price".

## "The agent says it's a good price"

Most buyers have heard this line. The listing has a number, the agent sounds sure, and the portal's estimate seems close enough.

But "close enough" to what? A number only means something next to the right comparison. Pick the wrong comparison and a high price can look cheap, or a fair one can look expensive.

Here is the method I use, with real sales from one Clementi condo.

## Step 1: Start with the same project, the same size, the last 12 months

Every private home sale in Singapore leaves a record with URA, once a caveat is lodged. Those records show the project, the size, the floor range, the price and the month.

Take a 721 sq ft unit at **Parc Clematis** in Clementi. In the 12 months to August 2026, seven units of that same size changed hands. They sold for between **$1,535,888 and $1,652,000**.

| What we're comparing | Price | Per sq ft |
|---|---|---|
| Lowest of the seven same-size sales | $1,535,888 | about $2,130 |
| Highest of the seven same-size sales | $1,652,000 | about $2,291 |
| An asking price of $1.75M (made up for this example) | $1,750,000 | about $2,427 |

So an asking price of $1.75 million would sit about **6% above the highest** of those seven sales. Not one recent buyer paid that much for this size.

That doesn't mean nobody will. A high floor, a better facing or a renovated unit can earn a premium. But now you know the question to ask: *what makes this unit worth more than every recent sale of the same size?*

## Step 2: Ignore the project-wide average

Here is the trap. Look at the same project across **all** sizes and the average sale comes to about **$1.98 million**.

Against $1.98M, a $1.75M asking price looks like a bargain. It isn't. That average includes three- and four-bedroom units that are much bigger. It tells you nothing about a 721 sq ft unit.

An average across sizes answers a question nobody asked.

## Step 3: Adjust for floor and stack, then look next door

Once you have the same-size sales, sort them by floor range. Higher floors often go for more in the same project, so a low-floor unit asking the top price deserves a closer look.

Only after that should you look at nearby projects. Pick ones of a similar age and tenure, and compare per square foot, not total price. Neighbours help you check whether the whole project is moving up or down. They don't replace the project's own sales.

## Step 4: Know when there isn't enough evidence

Now take a small freehold block nearby. In the same 12 months it recorded **one** sale.

One sale is not a market. It could have been a family selling to a relative, a unit in poor condition, or a buyer who simply loved the view. Build your price on that one number and you are guessing.

When the evidence is thin, I say so. Then I widen the search carefully: a longer period, similar sizes, comparable blocks nearby. And I show you which of those sales I used and why.

## What to avoid

**1. Don't compare against the whole project's average.**
It mixes small and large units. The size you're buying is the only size that matters.

**2. Don't trust a single number with no working shown.**
A portal estimate or an agent's "good price" is a starting point. Ask which sales it is based on.

**3. Don't treat one recent sale as proof.**
With fewer than a handful of sales, say "not enough evidence" out loud, and widen the search before you make an offer.

**4. Don't forget the date.**
A sale from two years ago tells you about two years ago. Recent sales come first.

## What to do instead

Before you view, or at least before you offer:

- **Write down the project, the size and the asking price.**
- **Pull every sale of that size in that project from the last 12 months.** Note the floor range and the month.
- **Work out the per sq ft range** and see where the asking price sits in it.
- **Ask what justifies any gap:** floor, facing, condition, layout.
- **If there are very few sales,** widen the search carefully and say so in your own notes.

## What would change this answer

The figures above are URA caveat records up to August 2026, and markets move. Caveats are lodged after a deal is done, so the newest sales can take a few weeks to appear. A unit with a special feature, such as a penthouse, a corner stack or a large private enclosed space, may not have true comparables at all. This method shows you where a price sits against real sales. It does not predict what any unit will sell for.

## Quick questions

**Where can I see recent condo sale prices in Singapore?**
URA publishes private residential transactions based on caveats lodged. You can search them by project on URA's website, and most property portals show the same records.

**Should I compare per square foot or total price?**
Compare per square foot when sizes differ, and total price when you have sales of the exact same size. Always compare the same size band first.

**Is the project's average price a good guide?**
No. It mixes small and large units. A 721 sq ft unit and a 1,200 sq ft unit in the same project sell at very different totals.

**What if there is only one recent sale?**
Then there isn't enough evidence to call the price fair or unfair. Widen the search to a longer period or similar nearby projects, and treat any conclusion with caution.
